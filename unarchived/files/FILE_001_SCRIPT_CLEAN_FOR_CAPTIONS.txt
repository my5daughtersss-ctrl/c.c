January 2023. Hyderabad, India.

A coffin arrives from Istanbul.

Inside is a man who was once heir to one of the greatest private fortunes on Earth. He went to Harrow. He went to Cambridge. At thirty-three, he was proclaimed the eighth Nizam. He inherited palaces, gold, jewels, and more than fourteen thousand people who depended on him.

And in 2005, his biographer tracked him down to a modest two-bedroom flat overlooking the Mediterranean.

Fifty-six years before this funeral, as many as a million people had filled this city to mourn his grandfather. The man TIME magazine had once called the richest in the world. A man who kept a diamond nearly twice the size of the Koh-i-Noor on his desk… as a paperweight.

So how does a fortune that large become impossible to keep?

And why did a London bank hold one million pounds of it, frozen and untouchable, for seventy-one years?

This is the tragic story of the Nizam of Hyderabad, and the fortune no one could hold on to.

To understand the fortune, you first have to understand the throne it sat on.

In the early eighteenth century, as the Mughal Empire began to break apart, one of its governors in southern India took the title Nizam and never gave the land back. His descendants ruled it for more than two hundred years. By the twentieth century, Hyderabad was a kingdom larger than many European countries, with its own currency and its own railway, and an arrangement with the British Empire: the Nizams stayed loyal to the Crown, and the Crown left them on their throne.

On the 29th of August, 1911, a twenty-five-year-old named Mir Osman Ali Khan inherited all of it. He became the seventh Nizam.

And then, in February 1937, the rest of the world found out how rich he was.

On the 22nd of February, TIME magazine put him on its cover and called him the richest man in the world. TIME estimated his fortune at one point four billion dollars, including a hundred and fifty million dollars in jewels and two hundred and fifty million dollars in gold bars.

In today's money, that is more than thirty billion dollars. In the middle of the Great Depression.

But look closely at that word: estimated. Nobody had audited this fortune. Nobody could. Much of it was treasure, not money: jewels in cellars, gold in storerooms, land, and palaces. It seems that even the Nizam himself did not know exactly what he owned.

And that is the first clue to everything that follows. A fortune you cannot count is a fortune you cannot easily protect.

Over the next eighty years, five forces would reshape it. Each one answers a different question. Who controlled it. What became untouchable. What was locked away. What came attached to it. And what was sold or fought over.

We'll take them one at a time. But first, the object that tells you everything about the man who owned it.

Picture this.

It is 1911. The old Nizam, Mahbub Ali Khan, has just died. His son is going through his father's belongings, in palaces so vast that some rooms have not been opened in years.

And inside one of his father's shoes, he finds something hidden.

A diamond. About one hundred and eighty-five carats. Nearly twice the size of the Koh-i-Noor, the most famous diamond in the British Crown Jewels.

It is called the Jacob Diamond. His father had bought it in a deal so bitter that it ended up in court. And at some point, the stone was hidden away, in a shoe.

And what did his son do with one of the largest diamonds on Earth?

He used it as a paperweight.

Hold on to that picture. A priceless stone, holding down papers on a desk. Because before this story ends, that same diamond will be somewhere its owner could never have imagined.

The diamond was only one piece of it. It was said that his pearls alone could have paved London's Piccadilly Circus. According to the writers Larry Collins and Dominique Lapierre, trucks loaded with gold sat in his garden, so heavy they had sunk into the mud, and jewels lay heaped on cellar floors like coal.

Some of those stories may have grown with each telling. But they all point to the same thing. This was wealth on a scale so large that the people around him had simply stopped trying to measure it.

And the man at the center of it was nothing like you'd expect.

You would expect the richest man in the world to dress like it.

He didn't.

By several accounts, the Nizam wore crumpled pajamas and a tattered fez, and ate from a tin plate. According to Collins and Lapierre, he wore the same fez for thirty-five years, offered visitors one cup of tea, one biscuit and one cigarette, and smoked the cigarette ends his guests left behind. His biographer John Zubrzycki writes that he saved on laundry by bathing in his clothes, and that when he died, The Times of London called him a miser.

And yet that is only half of the man.

The same Nizam founded Osmania University, teaching in Urdu rather than English. He gave to the Hindu university at Banaras and the Muslim university at Aligarh. In the First World War, he gave millions of pounds to Britain's war effort, and Britain named him its Faithful Ally. He paid for British warplanes in that war too, for a squadron that carried Hyderabad's name, Number 110. And in the Second World War, he did it again. He paid for Spitfires. Another Royal Air Force squadron, Number 152, flew as the Hyderabad Squadron.

Think about what that money was buying. Not luxury. Loyalty. Every gift to the Crown was a reminder to London that this was a friend worth keeping on his throne. For thirty years, that bargain held.

And in 1947, when Princess Elizabeth was about to marry Philip Mountbatten, the Nizam sent instructions to Cartier in London: the princess should choose her own wedding present. She chose two pieces. A floral diamond tiara, and a platinum necklace set with around three hundred diamonds. The tiara was later taken apart, its diamonds reset into another royal tiara. But the necklace survived. The young Queen wore it in her first official portraits. Decades later, the Princess of Wales would wear it too.

It's a strange thought. Of all the Nizam's treasures, one of the best preserved today isn't in Hyderabad at all. It's in the royal collection in London.

So which man was he? The miser who saved cigarette ends, or the king who bought warplanes and royal diamonds?

The honest answer is both. He was frugal with small things and lavish with anything that bought loyalty or prestige. And above all, he kept control of everything himself. That habit would matter enormously once he was gone.

Because he had already made the decision that would shape the fortune's future.

He had an eldest son, Azam Jah, who should have been his heir. And he passed him over.

According to accounts at the time, Azam Jah had a reputation as a gambler and a big spender, though some historians caution that those descriptions reflect the views of British officials as much as the man himself. We don't know the full truth. But we know the Nizam's choice. He named his grandson, a boy called Mukarram Jah, as the future eighth Nizam.

Remember that boy. One day, all of this would land on him.

Because the world that had made this fortune possible was about to end. And it would take about four days.

In August 1947, Britain left India. The subcontinent was divided into two new nations, India and Pakistan, and more than five hundred princely states were expected to join one of them.

The Nizam refused.

He wanted Hyderabad to stand alone, as it had for two hundred years. And from his point of view, the logic was simple. His wealth had always depended on one thing: being the ruler of the land it came from. Joining another country meant handing that power to someone else.

So he fought for it the way a rich man would. With lawyers. He hired one of Britain's most respected, Sir Walter Monckton, as his constitutional adviser. According to one account, Monckton urged him to take his case to the United Nations. And in November 1947, Hyderabad and India signed a standstill agreement: one year to keep things as they were while they negotiated.

But Hyderabad sat in the very heart of the new India, and the talks went nowhere. Inside the state, tensions boiled over. An armed militia called the Razakars, loyal to the old order, carried out attacks. A peasant uprising fought its own war against the landlords. Each side accused the other of breaking the agreement.

And on the 13th of September, 1948, Indian troops crossed the border.

India called it a police action. It was code-named Operation Polo.

On the 17th of September, the Nizam announced a ceasefire. The next day, his forces formally surrendered. A kingdom that had lasted more than two centuries was gone in about four days.

Let's pause here, because this part of the story isn't about money.

In the violence during and after those days, many people lost their lives. A committee appointed by the Indian government itself, the Sunderlal Committee, estimated that between twenty-seven thousand and forty thousand people died. Its report stayed confidential for decades, and historians still argue over what happened and who was responsible. What is clear is that ordinary people in Hyderabad, Hindu and Muslim alike, paid a far heavier price than the man in the palace.

The Nizam kept his palaces and much of his private wealth. But the answer to our first question had changed forever. He no longer controlled the state that had produced his fortune. From now on, other people would decide what he kept.

And in the chaos of those final days, one transfer was made that would outlive almost everyone involved.

Remember this name: Moin Nawaz Jung. He had been Hyderabad's finance minister.

Picture a London bank in the autumn of 1948. Polished counters. Ledgers. Clerks in dark suits, still working by the rules of an empire that was disappearing around them. Thousands of miles away, Hyderabad has just fallen. Here, it's an ordinary Monday morning.

The 20th of September, 1948.

The fighting was over. The Nizam had surrendered. And in London, at Westminster Bank, a transfer went through.

One million, seven thousand, nine hundred and forty pounds and nine shillings was moved out of an account held by the government of Hyderabad, into an account in the name of Pakistan's High Commissioner in London, Habib Ibrahim Rahimtoola. The transfer had been arranged by Moin Nawaz Jung.

Pakistan's position was that the money was payment for weapons. The Nizam's position was that it had been moved without his authority, and he wanted it back.

So whose money was it?

No one could agree. And the bank was caught in the middle. On one side, a new nation claiming the money as payment. On the other, a fallen king claiming it had been taken without his permission. Hand it to the wrong side, and the bank itself could be liable. So it did the only thing it could. It froze the account.

And there the money sat. As the Nizam grew old. As Britain's empire dissolved. As Westminster Bank itself became NatWest. The interest quietly piled up while governments and families argued over it.

Think about what that means. This was money anyone could name, to the shilling. Everyone knew exactly where it was. And for decades, no one could touch it. Wealth that still exists, but can no longer be used. And it would take seventy-one years, and a High Court judge, to open that vault.

After 1948, the richest man in the world became a figurehead.

From 1950, he served as Rajpramukh, the ceremonial head of Hyderabad State, bound by the advice of elected ministers. In 1956 the state itself was broken up and redrawn, and even its name disappeared from the map.

In return for giving up his kingdom, he received what India called a privy purse: an allowance of five million rupees a year, tax-free.

At the exchange rates of the day, that was roughly a million dollars a year, paid by the Indian government in return for the kingdom he had given up. A fortune by most standards. But notice what had changed. For two centuries, the Nizams had collected the revenues of a kingdom. Now the Nizam collected an allowance, paid by a government he did not control, which that government could one day decide to stop.

Meanwhile, his real wealth was becoming harder and harder to use. In 1951 and 1952, he placed his famous jewels into two trusts, the Nizam's Jewellery Trust and a supplemental trust, intended to keep them together for the family. It was a sensible step. But a trust is a set of rules, and rules cut both ways. Jewels placed in a trust usually can't be sold on a whim when someone needs money. They're controlled by its terms and its trustees, not by any one member of the family.

And his palaces were full of treasure, but palaces cost money to run. Servants, guards, relatives and retainers all had to be paid, whether the money came in or not.

This is the part that's easiest to miss. Treasure is not the same as cash. A diamond on a desk doesn't pay a single salary. Gold in a storeroom pays no bills until you sell it. And the more of your wealth is locked in jewels, buildings and trusts, the less of it you can actually spend.

And here's where this story touches almost every family. The Nizam's jewels were locked in trusts that would take decades of lawyers to unwind. Most families never need anything that complicated. There's a famous story from these years that you may have heard. In 1965, the Nizam is said to have donated five thousand kilograms of gold to India.

It didn't happen that way. According to records released under India's Right to Information Act, there is no record of any such donation. What he actually did was invest around four hundred and twenty-five kilograms of gold in a national defence gold bond scheme. A huge sum. But an investment, not a gift, and less than a tenth of the legend.

On the 24th of February, 1967, the seventh Nizam died at his palace, King Kothi, aged eighty.

Hyderabad came out to say goodbye. Police estimated eight hundred thousand mourners. Some estimates put it near a million. The man The Times would call a miser, the ruler who had lost his kingdom nineteen years earlier, was still mourned by a city. Whatever else he had lost, he had not lost them.

And on the 6th of April, his grandson was crowned in his place.

Mukarram Jah was thirty-three years old. And his life story already sounded like something from a novel. His mother, Princess Durru Shehvar, was the daughter of the last Caliph of the Ottoman Empire. He'd been born in France, educated at Harrow and Cambridge, and trained at Sandhurst. He was polished, private, and thoroughly European. The boy his grandfather had chosen over his own son.

What he had never been trained to do was run a fortune.

Now imagine you are him.

You've just inherited one of the great fortunes of the world, and with it, everyone who depends on it. Where would you even begin?

According to The New York Times, he took on more than fourteen thousand seven hundred staff and dependants. Among them, by the historian William Dalrymple's account, were forty-two of his grandfather's concubines, and around three thousand bodyguards.

An inheritance isn't only what you receive. It's also everything that comes attached to it. Every one of those people expected to be housed, fed or paid. And according to The New York Times, when he looked more closely, around four thousand of the names being paid did not seem to exist at all.

The wealth itself was tied up in trusts that would be contested for years. And, again according to The New York Times, among the first people to take him to court were his own father and his own aunt.

He later summed up his inheritance in four words.

"I inherited a scrapyard."

It's easy to judge him for what came next. But think about what he had been handed: a fortune nobody could measure, a household the size of a town, obligations he hadn't chosen, and a family ready to sue. His biographer, John Zubrzycki, put it bluntly: he had no inkling whatsoever of anything financial.

Then, in 1971, India removed what little certainty remained. The 26th Amendment to its constitution abolished the privy purses and the titles of every former ruler. The allowance was gone. Officially, so was the Nizam.

And so the young man who had inherited a kingdom's obligations decided to start again, on the other side of the world.

Picture this. The red dust of Western Australia. A coastline so empty you can drive for hours without seeing another person. And in the middle of it, a sheep station spread across hundreds of thousands of acres.

In the early 1970s, the heir to one of the richest fortunes on Earth bought Murchison House Station, along with a grand house in Perth.

He threw himself into it. According to The New York Times, he even bought a gold mine and an amphibious tank.

But the station lost money. To fund his life there, he sold family jewels. He married five times, and as Dalrymple put it, every marriage added to a growing pile of litigation. Settlements, lawsuits and failed ventures ate into what was left.

Look at it the way an accountant would. On one side were assets that were hard to turn into cash quickly: jewels, palaces, land. On the other was a steady drain that never stopped: a household to support, legal fees, settlements, and a station that lost money. And much of what remained was controlled by trusts and courts, not by him. When money goes out every month and comes in only when something is sold, you keep selling. The records don't give us a full balance sheet. But the pattern is clear.

And back in Hyderabad, with their owner on the other side of the world, the great palaces stood half empty. According to Dalrymple and others, they were looted, piece by piece.

Then came the sale that tells you more about this story than almost anything else.

Remember the jewel trusts, set up in the early 1950s to protect the family's treasures? By the 1970s, the family and the government were negotiating over them. The talks dragged on for years. There were injunctions, appeals, and finally a ruling from India's Supreme Court in 1994.

And in 1995, after more than twenty years of legal battles, the Government of India bought one hundred and seventy-three pieces of the Nizam's jewellery, for around two hundred and eighteen crore rupees.

Two hundred and eighteen crore rupees is an enormous amount of money. But it arrived only after twenty years of lawyers, and the treasures themselves now belonged to the nation. The protection the old Nizam had built around his jewels had kept them together. It had not kept them in the family.

Among them was a diamond of about one hundred and eighty-five carats.

The Jacob Diamond. The stone found in a dead man's shoe. The paperweight.

Today it no longer sits on anyone's desk. It's owned by the Government of India, and held in a Reserve Bank of India vault. The symbol of unlimited wealth had become something else: proof that owning a treasure is not the same as controlling a fortune.

In 1996, the sheep station was sold to pay his debts, and the eighth Nizam moved to Turkey, his mother's homeland.

Not one catastrophe, then. A long, slow leak: sales, settlements and lawsuits, one after another.

But there was one part of the fortune still left. In London, the money was still waiting.

For decades, the frozen account sat in London while the world changed around it. Pakistan claimed it. India claimed it. The Nizam's heirs claimed it. The old Nizam died in 1967. The empire the bank had served disappeared. And still, the money waited.

Picture the courtroom. The case is about a single transfer, made seventy years earlier, in a kingdom that no longer existed. And yet every detail matters: who signed what, on whose authority, two days after which surrender.

And on the 2nd of October, 2019, in London's High Court, Mr Justice Marcus Smith delivered his judgment.

The money, he ruled, had belonged to the seventh Nizam all along. His heirs, Mukarram Jah and his brother Muffakham Jah, together with India, were entitled to it. Pakistan's arguments failed.

And the one million pounds moved two days after a surrender?

After seventy-one years, it had grown to around thirty-five million pounds.

It's a remarkable number, but it isn't the answer to what happened to the whole fortune. It's one fund. And it came at a price of its own. Seventy-one years of claims and counterclaims meant lawyers on every side. A few months later, the court ordered Pakistan to pay, up front, sixty-five percent of the other sides' legal costs. Even a victory, in this family, arrived with a bill attached. And even this victory didn't end the arguments. Within days of the ruling, another grandson of the seventh Nizam, Najaf Ali Khan, said that around a hundred and twenty relatives deserved a share.

Meanwhile, Hyderabad itself was reclaiming the past. Mukarram Jah's first wife, Princess Esra, had returned and led the restoration of the Chowmahalla Palace, which won a UNESCO heritage award in 2010. The Falaknuma Palace, high on a hill above the city, was restored and leased to the Taj hotel group. Today, anyone can book a room there.

And in January 2023, the eighth Nizam died in Istanbul, aged eighty-nine. His body was flown home, and on the 18th of January he was buried in Hyderabad beside his ancestors. The coffin from the beginning of this story.

Even now, it isn't over. A grandson, Azmet Jah, was named the ninth Nizam, a title the Indian state does not recognize and other members of the family dispute.

So what was actually lost?

The diamond survived. The palaces survived. Even the money trapped in a London bank survived long enough for a judge to decide who owned it.

What did not survive was the world that had made the fortune possible: the throne that produced it, and the power to control it.

Maybe that's the real lesson of this file. A fortune is only as strong as the system that protects it. An heir needs training, not just a title. And a family without clear rules doesn't inherit wealth. It inherits arguments.
