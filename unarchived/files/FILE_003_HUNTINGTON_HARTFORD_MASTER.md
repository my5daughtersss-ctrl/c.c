## SCRIPT

### [COLD OPEN]

December 2004. The Bahamas.

A magazine reporter is looking for a man most of New York assumed had simply vanished.

She finds him at the age of ninety-three. He is frail. He has spent roughly ten years as a recluse. And the people closest to him say he has spent much of that decade fighting a drug habit that began when he was well past sixty.

Now, here is what makes this strange.

This man was once called one of the richest young men in America. His grandfather's company was, for decades, the largest retailer in the United States. He grew up with butlers, with mansions, with a fortune that Forbes would later estimate at around ninety million dollars.

He bought an island in the Bahamas. He renamed it Paradise.

Today, on that island, stands Atlantis — one of the most famous resorts on Earth. Millions of tourists have walked across it. Almost none of them know his name.

Because by the time this reporter found him, he had lost at least eighty million dollars of that fortune. He had built a museum that closed in five years, a magazine that bled money with every issue, and a paradise he sold for a fraction of what it would become.

And yet — through all of it — there was one pot of money he never touched. One locked drawer in the family file.

Why did a man who lost almost everything never open it?

This is the tragic story of Huntington Hartford — the grocery heir who owned Paradise, and lost it.

### DOCUMENT I — THE GROCERY THRONE

To understand the man, you have to understand the money. And the money began with tea.

In the middle of the nineteenth century, in New York City, a merchant named George Gilman built a small business selling tea. A young man from Maine named George Huntington Hartford went to work for him. Over the following decades, Hartford rose from employee to partner, and then, after Gilman retired, to the man who ran the whole operation.

The company took a grand name: The Great Atlantic & Pacific Tea Company. Most Americans would come to know it by three letters. A&P.

And A&P did something no one had quite done before. It took the corner grocery and turned it into a machine. Standard stores. Low prices. Cash only. Thousands of locations, all run the same way.

By around 1930, A&P had something like fifteen thousand stores. ⟦v2.1⟧ For six decades, it was the largest grocery retailer in America — and until 1965, the largest retailer of any kind in the country.

Picture what that means in money. Every loaf of bread, every can of coffee, every pound of butter, in fifteen thousand stores, every single day — and a share of it flowing back to one family.

The founder had sons. Two of them, John and George, would run A&P and become among the most powerful businessmen in the country. A third son, Edward, took a different road.

Edward Hartford was an inventor. He perfected a shock absorber for automobiles, founded his own company to make it, and married a socialite from an old Charleston family named Henrietta Guerard Pollitzer.

On April 18th, 1911, in New York City, they had a son. They named him after his grandfather: George Huntington Hartford the Second.

Everyone called him Huntington.

He had an older sister, Josephine. He had a mother with social ambitions. He had a father who built things with his hands. And he had, waiting for him, a share of one of the great fortunes of the age.

But in 1922, when Huntington was just eleven years old, his father died.

Hold on to that picture. An eleven-year-old boy who has just lost the one man in his family who built something of his own. Because for the rest of his life, Huntington Hartford would try — again and again, at enormous cost — to prove that he could build something too.

After his father's death, Huntington became one of the heirs to the estate his grandfather had left. And here is a detail worth noting. Much of what came to him did not arrive as a pile of cash. It came, over time, as income from family trusts.

Remember that word. Trust. It will come back at the very end of this story.

His mother, now a wealthy widow, sent him away to St. Paul's School in New Hampshire, one of the most exclusive boarding schools in America. She herself would later marry an Italian prince and become Princess Pignatelli.

The family's world was Newport summers, private railcars of social obligation, and the quiet assumption that a Hartford boy would grow up, take his place, and keep the machine running.

But Huntington Hartford did not want to keep the machine running.

He wanted something much harder to buy.

### DOCUMENT II — THE BOY WHO WOULDN'T SELL GROCERIES

In 1931, while he was still a student at Harvard, Huntington married for the first time. Her name was Mary Lee Epling, a young society woman. It did not last. They divorced in 1939, and she would go on to marry the Hollywood star Douglas Fairbanks Jr.

That would become a pattern. Huntington Hartford would marry four times. Every one of those marriages would end in divorce.

He graduated from Harvard in 1934. And then he did the thing his family expected. He went to work for A&P.

Briefly.

Very briefly.

Because the young heir quickly discovered that he had no interest at all in the grocery business. He did not want to count cans. He did not want to argue about the price of coffee. He did not want to be the third generation of a family famous for selling things cheaply.

He wanted to be an artist. A writer. A producer. A patron. A man whose name would be remembered not for groceries — but for culture.

And this is where you have to understand the trap he was in.

Most young men who dream of art have no money. They struggle, they starve, they get rejected, and over time they either get better or they quit.

Huntington Hartford never had to struggle. He never had to starve. When a door didn't open, he could simply buy the building.

So he never learned the one thing that failure teaches everyone else.

When to stop.

Let's pause here for a moment, because it's easy to laugh at a rich man burning money. But think about what he was actually asking for. He didn't want more money. He already had more than he could spend. What he wanted was respect. He wanted the art world, the theatre world, the critics, the serious people, to look at him and see a man — not a bank account.

And that is the one thing money cannot buy. The harder you try to buy it, the less you get.

He was about to spend the next thirty years proving it.

### DOCUMENT III — THE PRICE OF BEING TAKEN SERIOUSLY

After the war, Huntington Hartford went west. To Hollywood.

And one night, at Ciro's — the famous nightclub on the Sunset Strip, where movie stars and gangsters drank side by side — he noticed a young woman selling cigarettes from a tray.

Her name was Marjorie Steele. She was a teenager from Reno, Nevada, working as a cigarette girl while she tried to become an actress.

He was in his late thirties. One of the richest men in America.

In 1949, shortly after her nineteenth birthday, they married.

And what did he do for his new wife? He made her a movie star. Or he tried to. He produced films for her to appear in, including a short drama called Hello Out There in 1949 ⟦v2.1⟧ — which was never even released — and a feature called Face to Face in 1952.

Neither one made either of them famous.

But Huntington wasn't finished with Hollywood. In 1954, he opened a theatre in Los Angeles and put his own name on it: the Huntington Hartford Theatre. For about a decade, it was one of the city's premier stages for Broadway-scale productions.

According to The Washington Post, he also funded an artists' foundation and colony in Los Angeles.

On paper, it looked like the life he wanted. A beautiful young wife. A theatre with his name over the door. Artists who depended on his generosity.

But the money only ever flowed one way. Out.

And then, in 1955, he did something that would define how the art world saw him for the rest of his life.

At that moment, the most fashionable art in America was abstract. Jackson Pollock's drips. Great fields of color. Paintings that, to many ordinary people, didn't look like anything at all.

Huntington Hartford hated it.

So he took out full-page advertisements in the newspapers — and attacked the entire modern art establishment.

Think about that. Imagine opening your morning paper and finding that an A&P heir has bought an entire page to tell the critics, the museums, and the most celebrated painters in the country that they were wrong.

Some ordinary readers cheered. The art world did not. To the people whose respect he wanted most, he had just announced himself as an enemy.

Nine years later he doubled down, with a book called Art or Anarchy? A Harvard Crimson review described it as linking modern painting to something like a communist plot.

He had wanted the critics to take him seriously.

Instead, they now had a reason to take him personally.

And still, the spending went on. He had written his own stage adaptation of Jane Eyre. At one point, the swashbuckling film star Errol Flynn was attached to play Mr. Rochester. Flynn didn't last. When the play finally reached Broadway in 1958, ⟦v2.1⟧ it closed after just fifty-two performances.

By 1960, his second marriage was over too.

Marjorie Steele, the cigarette girl he had tried to turn into a star, walked away. They had two children together, a daughter named Catherine and a son named John. Keep those names in mind.

Now — before we follow Huntington Hartford to the island that would make and break him — I want to mention something quickly.

Almost every family in this archive lost money in the same few ways: no plan, no paperwork, the wrong people in charge of the wrong accounts. So I put together a free, three-page checklist — the Family Legacy Checklist. It covers the simple steps these families missed, so yours doesn't have to. It's free, and the link is in the description.

And if you want the full story of this man's life, the definitive biography is Squandered Fortune by Lisa Gubernick. That's linked below as well.

Because now, we're going to the Bahamas.

### DOCUMENT IV — HOG ISLAND

Just off the coast of Nassau, across a narrow stretch of harbor, there was a long, thin island of scrubland, mangroves, and white sand.

It had an unglamorous name. Hog Island.

It belonged to a Swedish industrialist, Axel Wenner-Gren, one of the richest men in the world. And around 1959, Huntington Hartford bought it from him — by most accounts, for about eleven million dollars.

In today's money, that's well over one hundred million.

And the first thing he did was change its name. A pig farm doesn't sell holidays. So Hog Island became Paradise Island.

Then he began to build.

In 1962, he opened a small, exquisite resort called the Ocean Club. Low buildings. Quiet gardens. The kind of place where very rich people go not to be seen.

And behind it, he created something even more extraordinary. He built terraced gardens and called them the Versailles Gardens — lined with classical statues, stepping down toward the sea.

At the top of those gardens, he placed a cloister. A real one. A medieval stone cloister, built centuries earlier in France.

Here's how he got it. Decades before, the newspaper tycoon William Randolph Hearst had bought that cloister, had it taken apart stone by stone, and shipped across the Atlantic. After Hearst, Hartford bought it from his estate and had it brought to Paradise Island.

But there was a problem. According to the accounts that survive, when the stones arrived, they weren't numbered. No one had a guide to how the pieces fit together. A sculptor reportedly spent about two years putting the building back together, piece by piece, by eye.

Hold that image. A man trying to assemble something beautiful from pieces that came with no instructions.

It is, in a way, the story of Huntington Hartford's entire life.

Because the Ocean Club had a fatal flaw.

It was beautiful. It was exclusive. And it was far too small to make money.

The sums simply didn't work. A handful of rooms, no matter how expensive, could never pay back what he was pouring into the island — roads, utilities, a harbor, staff. ⟦v2⟧ The Washington Post would later put his investment in Paradise Island at around thirty million dollars.

In the Bahamas in the early 1960s, everyone knew where the real money was.

A casino.

A casino would bring crowds. A casino would fill rooms. A casino would turn his beautiful, money-losing dream into a machine — the kind of machine his grandfather had built.

There was just one problem.

He didn't have a gambling licence.

And in the Bahamas of that era, the path to a casino licence ran through a political world that, in later years, would be investigated by commissions and written about in exposés. Organized crime had deep roots in the casino business of the Caribbean. The men who got licences tended to be men who knew how the system worked.

Huntington Hartford was a lot of things.

He was not one of those men.

So he waited. And he spent. And he waited.

And the island that was supposed to prove he could build something of his own began, very quietly, to eat him alive.

### DOCUMENT V — THREE MILLION DOLLARS

By the mid-1960s, Huntington Hartford's empire was bleeding from every direction.

We'll get to the museum and the magazine in a moment. But first — the island. Because what happened next is the single most painful decision in his entire story.

In January 1966, Huntington Hartford announced a deal.

He was selling control of Paradise Island to a company called the Mary Carter Paint Company.

Yes. A paint company.

Mary Carter Paint had already been buying land in the Bahamas, and now it wanted the island. Under the deal, according to one detailed account of the sale, Hartford would get three million dollars in cash. The paint company would take over a nine-million-dollar mortgage. And Hartford would keep a twenty-five percent interest in the operation.

Mary Carter took the controlling seventy-five percent.

Three million dollars in cash.

For an island he had paid around eleven million for — and spent, by ⟦v2⟧ The Washington Post's estimate, about thirty million developing.

Now, why would he do it?

Because he needed the money. Because the island was losing it. Because, by most accounts, he simply couldn't make the casino happen — and without the casino, Paradise was a beautiful hole in the sea that he kept shoveling cash into.

So he let go.

And here is where the story turns cruel.

That same year — 1966 — within months of Hartford selling control, the island's new owners received exactly what he had never been able to get.

Permission for a casino.

⟦v2⟧ Let that sink in. The thing he had waited years for. The one thing that could have turned his island into a fortune. It arrived for the new owners almost as soon as he had signed it away.

How did the paint company manage it so quickly? That question has been argued about for decades. There have been stories of political payments. There have been claims about mob influence. There have been counter-claims that the company resisted the mob entirely. We don't know the full truth, and I won't pretend we do.

But we know what happened next.

The Mary Carter Paint Company became a casino company. It changed its name to Resorts International. It built a bridge from Nassau to Paradise Island. It opened the casino. And the tourists came.

Years later, Resorts International would pass into the hands of the television host Merv Griffin. And in 1994, a South African hotel magnate named Sol Kerzner bought the Paradise Island resorts for about one hundred and twenty-five million dollars — and turned them into Atlantis.

Today, Atlantis is one of the most famous resorts in the world — thousands of rooms, water parks, aquariums, casino floors, and millions of visitors.

All of it standing on the island Huntington Hartford named. The island he had sold control of for three million dollars in cash.

He had built the dream. Other people collected the fortune.

⟦v2⟧ And that twenty-five percent he'd kept? According to The Washington Post, Resorts International eventually bought him out for one million dollars.

And this was the moment, more than any other, when the boy who wanted to prove he could build something like his grandfather had finally done it — and then given it away right before it paid off.

But the island wasn't the only fire burning.

### DOCUMENT VI — THE LOLLIPOP BUILDING

While Paradise Island was swallowing money in the Bahamas, Huntington Hartford was spending just as fast in New York.

In 1961, he launched a glossy magazine called Show, devoted to the arts and entertainment. It was meant to be elegant, intelligent, a rival to the great society magazines of the past.

⟦v2.1⟧ It had readers. But according to Time magazine, it was losing about one hundred thousand dollars an issue. When it first folded in 1964, it had cost him at least six million dollars.

And that wasn't even his most expensive dream.

At 2 Columbus Circle, at the southwest corner of Central Park, Huntington Hartford built a museum. He hired the architect Edward Durell Stone, and the result was a strange, narrow white marble tower, lifted off the street on a row of slender columns.

He called it the Gallery of Modern Art. It opened on March 21st, 1964.

And remember — this was a man who had spent years attacking modern art. His museum would show modern art the way he believed it should be: figurative, readable, beautiful. A deliberate challenge to the Museum of Modern Art, just a few blocks away.

The building cost him around seven point four million dollars — in today's money, something like seventy-five million.

And the critics were waiting.

The architecture critic Ada Louise Huxtable described the building as "a die-cut Venetian palazzo on lollipops." The name stuck. To New York, it became the Lollipop Building.

Once again, he had spent a fortune trying to win the respect of the serious people. And once again, the serious people laughed.

Among the treasures he had gathered was a massive painting by Salvador Dalí, The Battle of Tetuan. The gallery even hosted a major Dalí exhibition. But the crowds and the money never came in the way he needed.

The museum lost money every year. He began quietly selling off parts of his collection. And in 1969, after only about five years, he gave up and handed the building to Fairleigh Dickinson University.

Count what he had lost by the end of the 1960s.

The island. The magazine. The museum. The theatre, which he'd also let go. ⟦v2⟧ Two marriages, and a third about to end.

And a reputation, among the people he most wanted to impress, as a rich man who didn't know when to stop.

He was not yet sixty years old.

And the worst was still ahead.

### DOCUMENT VII — THE FOURTH WIFE

In 1962, Huntington had married for the third time, to Diane Brown. They had a daughter, Juliet. That marriage ended in 1970.

Then, in the mid-1970s, he married for the fourth and final time. Her name was Elaine Kay.

And this is the marriage that his obituaries would point to as the beginning of the end.

According to The Washington Post, it was during this fourth marriage that Huntington Hartford was introduced to cocaine, amphetamines, and Quaaludes.

He was in his sixties.

Let's pause here for a moment, because this is where it would be easy to make Elaine Kay the villain of this story. That's how some accounts tell it. A rich, aging man, a younger wife, the drugs, the decline.

But let's be fair to everyone in the file. Huntington Hartford was a grown man. He had been making his own expensive choices for forty years. Nobody forced him to buy an island he couldn't run, or a museum the critics hated, or a magazine that lost money every issue. The drugs may have started in that marriage. But the pattern — reaching for the next thing to fill the empty space — had started long before.

The marriage ended in divorce in 1981.

The drugs did not end with it.

And as the money ran down, the man withdrew. The grand parties stopped. The famous friends stopped calling. The tabloid darling of the 1950s slowly disappeared from New York society.

Remember the dazzling Dalí? In 1984, The Battle of Tetuan went under the hammer at Sotheby's in New York.

And then came the losses that no money could fix.

Remember Catherine — his daughter with Marjorie Steele? She died in 1988. She was only in her late thirties.

His obituaries would record that two of his children died before him. ⟦v2⟧ One of them, according to Vanity Fair, was a son he had supported for years but never legally acknowledged. Edward Barton took his own life in 1967. He was still in his twenties.

We don't know, from the public record, everything that happened inside that family. We don't know what those children carried, or what they needed from their father that he couldn't give. And it isn't our place to guess.

But we do know this. A man who had spent his whole life trying to build something lasting outlived two of the people he had actually created.

In 1992, Huntington Hartford filed for bankruptcy.

The grandson of the man who built the largest retailer in America. The heir to an estimated ninety million dollars.

Bankrupt.

By Forbes's estimate, he had lost at least eighty million of it. On an island, a museum, a magazine, a theatre, plays, films, and a lifetime of trying to buy the one thing he could never buy.

He disappeared into nearly a decade of isolation in New York.

And that's how this story should have ended. A forgotten old man, alone, the fortune gone.

But it didn't end there.

Because there was still one thing in the file that nobody had opened.

### DOCUMENT VIII — THE LOCKED DRAWER

In the early 2000s, his daughter Juliet — his child with Diane Brown — came for him.

By her own account, she rescued her father from roughly ten years as a recluse. And she took him somewhere he hadn't truly lived in decades.

Back to the Bahamas.

Not to Paradise Island. That belonged to other people now. Instead, they settled in Lyford Cay, an exclusive gated community on the western end of New Providence.

And this is where Vanity Fair found him in 2004, at ninety-three — the reporter from the start of our story.

And this is where the strangest detail in the whole file comes to light.

Because Huntington Hartford was not poor.

Remember the word we told you to hold on to? Trust.

Juliet told Vanity Fair that her father still had around eleven million dollars — held in a trust fund he had never touched.

Think about that. This was a man who bought an island, built a museum, launched a magazine, produced plays and films, and spent at least eighty million dollars. A man who had declared bankruptcy.

And all that time, there was a trust he had never emptied.

Bloomberg would report that he lived out his final years in comfort, in a seaside villa in Lyford Cay worth around six million dollars — supported by that one trust fund he had never exhausted.

How is that possible?

We don't have the trust documents themselves. So I can't tell you the exact terms. But this is exactly how certain kinds of family trusts are designed to work. When money is held in a trust, it isn't simply yours to spend. A trustee controls it. The rules are written in advance. And in many cases, the beneficiary can receive the income — but can't reach in and take the principal.

So whatever the precise terms, the result was clear.

Huntington Hartford could lose an island. He could lose a museum. He could lose a magazine, four marriages, and eighty million dollars.

But he couldn't lose the money he wasn't allowed to touch.

The grandfather who built A&P had never met the man his grandson would become. But the family's structure — the trusts, the rules, the locked drawer — protected that grandson from himself in a way nothing else in his life ever had.

When Vanity Fair asked him about the fortune he had lost, he said he didn't regret it. All along, he said, he'd been trying to create something beautiful.

You can decide for yourself whether you believe him.

### DOCUMENT IX — PARADISE, REMEMBERED

On May 19th, 2008, Huntington Hartford died of natural causes at his home in Lyford Cay. He was ninety-seven years old, and Juliet was there.

He had outlived his fortune. He had outlived two of his children. He had outlived almost everyone who ever laughed at him.

That same year, the building he'd built at 2 Columbus Circle — the Lollipop Building — reopened with a brand-new facade as the Museum of Arts and Design. The white marble skin and the famous lollipop columns, the face New York had mocked, were gone.

⟦v2⟧ His theatre in Hollywood is now the Ricardo Montalbán Theatre.

And Paradise Island? Today, Atlantis rises over it.

But if you leave the casino floors and the water slides behind, and walk to the quieter side of the island, you'll find a small, elegant resort. The Ocean Club — the hotel he opened in 1962 — still stands. One wing of it is named after him: the Hartford Wing.

And beyond it, up the terraced Versailles Gardens, past the old statues, there's the cloister. The medieval stones that arrived with no numbers. The building somebody had to put back together piece by piece, by eye.

Couples get married there now. Tourists take photos. And almost none of them know the name of the man who brought it there.

Huntington Hartford spent his life trying to assemble something beautiful from pieces that came with no instructions.

Most of it fell apart.

But that cloister is still standing.

So what do we learn from a man who inherited one of the great fortunes in America and spent it chasing respect?

Maybe this. Money can buy almost everything except the thing you want most. Talent doesn't come with an inheritance. And the most important thing his family ever left him wasn't the money he could spend.

It was the money he couldn't.

### [FILE LESSON]

Which brings us to today's File Lesson.

Huntington Hartford's last years were paid for by a trust that kept part of his fortune out of his own hands. Whatever its exact terms, the principle matters for ordinary families too.

A trust is a legal arrangement where one person, the trustee, holds and manages money or property for someone else, the beneficiary, under written rules. Many families use trusts so that an inheritance is paid out over time, or only for certain purposes, rather than as one lump sum.

Some trusts include what's called a spendthrift provision. In many places, that clause can limit a beneficiary's ability to sign away their future payments, and can make it harder for many creditors to reach the money before it's paid out. The rules vary a lot by state and country, and there are exceptions.

The point isn't distrust. It's protection — against bad luck, bad advice, bad marriages, or, sometimes, a beneficiary's own worst instincts.

If you're leaving money to someone you love, ask yourself: would a lump sum help them, or hurt them? And then speak with an estate attorney.
