The Tragic Fate of Edith Rockefeller's Children: The Heiress Who Lost It All

[COLD OPEN]

In August 1932, a woman lay dying in a hotel suite in Chicago. She was 59 years old.

Her father was the richest man in America. Her husband had been the heir to one of the greatest industrial fortunes of his age. She had lived in a mansion on Lake Shore Drive and owned a palace on Lake Michigan she barely set foot in. She had buried two of her five children before either could learn to read. She had spent eight years in Switzerland as a patient of Carl Jung, and she had paid James Joyce to keep writing.

And now, at the end, she was living in a rented hotel suite, and most of the fortune was gone.

Her father was still alive that summer. He was 93. For his entire adult life, he had written down every penny he ever spent. But there was one account he could never balance. His daughter's.

By the end of this file, you'll know where the money went, who was standing beside her when it disappeared, and why the richest family in America didn't stop it.

This is the tragic story of Edith Rockefeller McCormick, the heiress who had everything.

[BRAND STING]

File number one. The Rockefeller Daughter.

[CHAPTER 1: THE LEDGER]

To understand Edith, you have to start with a little book.

In 1855, a sixteen-year-old boy in Cleveland, Ohio, got his first job as an assistant bookkeeper. He bought a small notebook, and in it he recorded every cent he earned and every cent he spent. A few cents for a newspaper. A little for church. Nothing was too small to write down. He would later call it Ledger A, and he kept it for the rest of his life like a sacred object.

That boy was John D. Rockefeller.

Fifteen years later, in 1870, he founded Standard Oil. By the end of the century, it would control the vast majority of the oil refined in the United States, and the boy with the little notebook would become the richest man in the country.

Edith was born into that story on August 31, 1872, in Cleveland. Her father was 33. Standard Oil was barely two years old, and already growing faster than anyone could measure.

Picture the house she grew up in. It wasn't a palace. Her mother, Laura Spelman Rockefeller, was a devout Baptist and a former schoolteacher. There was no theater in that house, no dancing, no card games. There was church on Sunday, prayer at the table, and the quiet, constant lesson that money was a responsibility given by God, and every dollar would one day have to be accounted for.

Edith was one of four surviving children. There was Bessie, the eldest. There was Alta. Then Edith. And finally the long-awaited son, John Junior, the heir. A fifth child, Alice, had died as a baby before Edith was born. It was a grief the family rarely spoke of.

And here is the detail that tells you everything about this family. The richest man in America paid his children for chores. According to his biographer, Ron Chernow, the Rockefeller children could earn two cents for every fly they killed, ten cents for sharpening pencils, and a few cents for every hour of music practice. Each child kept a small account book of their own and recorded every coin, just like their father.

Hold on to that picture. A little girl in a long dress, bent over a tiny notebook, carefully writing down two cents for a fly. The daughter of a man worth millions, learning that money must always be counted.

That little book is going to come back in this story.

Of all the Rockefeller children, Edith was the one who didn't quite fit. She was sharp, proud, and fiercely intelligent. She devoured books. She learned languages. Where her sisters were obedient, Edith asked questions. Where her brother was groomed to be careful, Edith wanted to be grand.

In 1884, the family moved to New York, to a brownstone on West 54th Street, and spent their summers at Forest Hill, a large estate outside Cleveland. Edith was growing up surrounded by more money than almost anyone alive, and yet she was raised as if every penny might be the last.

Some children raised that way grow up afraid to spend. Edith would grow up to do the opposite.

[CHAPTER 2: TWO FORTUNES]

In the 1890s, the great American fortunes didn't just grow. They married each other.

Harold Fowler McCormick was the son of Cyrus McCormick, the man whose mechanical reaper had changed farming across America. The McCormick family company in Chicago would later become the core of International Harvester, one of the largest industrial companies in the nation. Harold was handsome, charming, and easygoing. He loved music, travel, and having a good time. In almost every way, he was the opposite of John D. Rockefeller.

On November 26, 1895, in New York, Edith Rockefeller married Harold McCormick. Both were 23.

The newspapers treated it like a royal wedding. Oil had married machinery. Two of the most powerful family names in America were now bound together, and so, in a sense, were their fortunes.

The young couple moved to Chicago and settled into a mansion at 1000 Lake Shore Drive. And it was here, away from her father's ledger and her mother's prayers, that Edith became someone new.

The girl who had once earned two cents for a fly now ran one of the most formal households in the city. Dinners were served with ceremony that would have impressed a European court. Footmen stood behind the chairs. Guests were expected to dress, to behave, and to understand that they were in the presence of Chicago royalty. Some people found her cold. Others found her magnificent. Almost everyone found her intimidating.

To put the scale of her world in perspective, the gifts Edith received from her father over the years ran into the tens of millions of dollars. In today's money, that's hundreds of millions.

Back in New York, her father watched this new life from a distance, and he didn't like what he saw. Over the years, his letters to Edith would return again and again to the same gentle, worried theme. Be careful. Be prudent. Remember what money is for.

Edith read those letters. And then, as we'll see, she largely went on doing exactly as she pleased.

But for a few short years, none of that mattered. Because the McCormick house was filling with children.

[CHAPTER 3: THE FIRST TWO GRAVES]

In 1896, Edith gave birth to a son. They named him John Rockefeller McCormick, after her father. A year later came a daughter, Editha. In 1898, another son, Harold Fowler McCormick Junior, whom everyone called Fowler.

Three children in three years. For a moment, the marriage of two great fortunes looked like the beginning of a dynasty.

Then the first grave came. Editha died as a baby. And the family barely had time to grieve before the second blow fell.

In the winter of 1900, little John, the boy named after his grandfather, came down with scarlet fever. Today, a course of antibiotics would most likely have saved him. In 1900, there were no antibiotics. All the money in America couldn't buy a cure that didn't exist yet.

On January 2, 1901, John Rockefeller McCormick died. He was four years old.

Let's pause here for a moment. Because this is the turning point of Edith's life, and it's easy to rush past it.

Think about what she had been taught since she was a child. That everything could be counted. Planned. Controlled. That discipline and order kept disaster away. And now she had buried two children before either one had learned to read, and none of the money, none of the order, none of the discipline had made the slightest difference.

Picture the nursery at 1000 Lake Shore Drive that winter. The small bed, the toys, the little clothes still folded in a drawer, waiting for a boy who would never wear them again. In a house with dozens of rooms and a staff to clean every one of them, there was now one room nobody wanted to enter.

People who knew Edith in those years described a change. The proud young hostess became more guarded, more formal, harder to reach. She held herself together in public the way her mother had taught her, with dignity and silence. A Rockefeller did not weep in front of the servants.

The grief didn't break her in a single day. It went somewhere deeper.

Edith and Harold turned their pain into something remarkable. In 1902, they founded the John McCormick Memorial Institute for Infectious Diseases in Chicago, a research center dedicated to fighting the kind of illness that had taken their son.

And it wasn't only Edith who was shaken. In 1901, the same year his grandson died, John D. Rockefeller founded the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research in New York. Some historians believe the little boy's death helped push him toward that decision. The grandson who carried his name never got to grow up. But the institute that bore his grandfather's name would go on to change modern medicine.

Hold on to that institute in Chicago, too. It's going to give this story one of its very few gifts.

Two more children came. Muriel was born in 1903, and Mathilde in 1905. Five children in all, two of them already gone.

But something in their mother had changed. Edith began to withdraw. She suffered phobias and panic attacks, and travel became difficult, then frightening. On top of the grief, she fought a long battle with tuberculosis of the kidney. Back then, people simply said Mrs. McCormick preferred to stay in.

From the outside, the McCormicks were still Chicago's golden couple. Behind the doors of 1000 Lake Shore Drive, the house had grown very quiet.

[CHAPTER 4: THE PALACE NAMED AFTER ZURICH]

If grief made Edith withdraw from people, it did not make her stop building.

Around 1908, the McCormicks began work on a country estate in Lake Forest, north of Chicago, on the bluffs above Lake Michigan. They hired one of the most celebrated architects in America, Charles A. Platt. What rose on that land was not a house. It was an Italian villa of 44 rooms, finished around 1912, a palace of terraces, stone stairways, and formal gardens stepping down toward the water.

They gave it an unusual name. Villa Turicum.

Turicum was the old Roman name for the city of Zurich, in Switzerland. At the time, it seemed like a simple, elegant reference to Europe. No one could have known what it would come to mean.

Remember that name.

Villa Turicum cost a fortune to build and another fortune to run. Gardeners, staff, upkeep, all of it paid, season after season. And here is the strange part. Edith, the woman for whom much of this splendor was created, would spend remarkably little time there.

And while the villa was rising in Lake Forest, the empire that paid for it was under attack.

For years, Americans had been reading about Standard Oil in the newspapers, and not kindly. A journalist named Ida Tarbell had spent years digging through the company's records, and her series exposing its methods had turned John D. Rockefeller into the most hated businessman in the country. Cartoonists drew Standard Oil as an octopus, its tentacles wrapped around the nation.

In May 1911, the United States Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil broken apart into dozens of separate companies. It looked like the end of an empire.

It wasn't. As shares in the new companies soared, Rockefeller became even richer than before. The government had tried to cut the fortune down to size, and instead it multiplied.

For Edith, that meant one thing. The money never seemed to run out. And when money never seems to run out, it becomes very easy to stop counting.

Meanwhile, the McCormicks poured money into Chicago's cultural life. They became leading patrons of the city's opera. Their names appeared on programs, boxes, and donor lists. To Chicago society, Edith was a queen with an unlimited treasury.

Back in New York, her father kept writing his careful letters. And somewhere in a drawer, the little account book of a girl who once counted pennies sat untouched.

By 1913, Edith was in crisis. The grief she had never fully faced, the fear of leaving home, the loneliness inside a marriage that was drifting apart, all of it had become too heavy to carry.

And so Edith Rockefeller McCormick, a woman who was afraid to travel, made the longest journey of her life.

She went to Zurich.

[CHAPTER 5: THE DOCTOR AND THE CHECKBOOK]

In 1913, Edith arrived in Switzerland to be treated by Carl Gustav Jung, at that time one of the most famous psychiatrists in the world.

She was meant to stay for a short course of treatment. She stayed for eight years.

Remember the name of her villa? Turicum. The old name of Zurich. The woman had built a palace named after the city she would eventually run away to.

Jung saw in Edith something no one else in her life had seen. Not a patient to be managed, not an heiress to be flattered, but a brilliant mind looking for meaning. Under his guidance, she didn't just receive analysis. She studied it. In time, she began to practice it herself, becoming one of the first American women to work as an analyst in the Jungian tradition.

For the first time in years, Edith felt alive.

Imagine what Zurich must have felt like to her. No reporters waiting outside the door. No Chicago society watching what she wore. Even her father's worried letters now had to cross an ocean to reach her. In Zurich, she wasn't the Rockefeller daughter or Mrs. Harold McCormick. She was a student of the mind, sitting in lecture rooms with some of the most original thinkers in Europe.

And then, in 1914, war broke out across Europe. Switzerland stayed neutral, an island of calm surrounded by fighting. Edith could easily have gone home. She didn't. Zurich had become the one place on earth where she felt she belonged.

And she paid for that feeling the way she paid for everything. Lavishly.

In 1916, Edith provided the money to found the Psychological Club of Zurich, a meeting place for Jung's circle of students and followers. She funded research, lectures, and translations. She supported young thinkers and artists.

One of them was a struggling Irish writer living in Zurich during the war, with failing eyesight and almost no money. His name was James Joyce. In February 1918, Edith began sending him a generous monthly stipend, enough to keep him and his family afloat while he worked on a novel called Ulysses.

Then, in October 1919, the money suddenly stopped.

Joyce was furious. He later claimed she cut him off because he refused to go into analysis with Jung or one of his followers. Whether that was the real reason, no one can say for certain. No clear explanation from Edith survives. It was the first sign of a pattern that would follow her for the rest of her life. Her money was a gift. But it was also a way of holding on.

And while Edith was in Zurich, her family back in America was falling apart.

In 1915, her mother, Laura, died. Edith was an ocean away, in a city at the edge of a world at war.

Her children were growing up partly in Switzerland, partly in America, raised by staff, by schools, and by distance. Harold traveled back and forth. He was also analyzed by Jung. But the marriage was quietly dying.

And her father? He kept writing his letters. Gentle, then firm. Worried about the spending. Worried about Jung. Worried about the money flowing out of America and into a city named Turicum.

It would be easy to make John D. Rockefeller the villain of this chapter. The cold, controlling father who couldn't understand his daughter. But look at him in 1915. A 75-year-old widower who had just buried his wife, whose daughter stayed an ocean away, whose grandchildren he barely saw, and whose money was disappearing into a world he didn't understand. He wasn't only counting dollars. He was counting losses.

And still, he didn't stop her. He kept paying.

Eight years in Zurich. Hundreds of thousands of dollars, by some estimates far more. And when Edith finally came home in 1921, she discovered that the life she had left behind was gone.

[CHAPTER 6: THE OPERA SINGER]

What Edith found waiting for her in America was the most public humiliation of her life.

Harold McCormick had fallen in love with someone else. Her name was Ganna Walska, a glamorous Polish singer with towering ambitions and, according to many critics, a voice that didn't quite match them.

In December 1921, Harold divorced Edith. The newspapers devoured every detail. The Rockefeller-McCormick marriage, once celebrated as the union of oil and machinery, ended in a courtroom in Chicago.

Eight months later, in August 1922, Harold married Ganna Walska. And then he did something that would become one of the great legends of American wealth. He poured enormous sums into promoting his new wife's opera career. The singing lessons, the performances, the publicity. Many people believe the story helped inspire the famous subplot of Citizen Kane, about a rich man who forces his untalented wife onto the opera stage.

And the children? They were following their mother into their own storms.

In 1922, Mathilde, just 17, announced she would marry Max Oser, her Swiss riding master. He was a 45-year-old widower. Her grandfather feared she would fall prey to a fortune hunter. And Edith herself went to court in Chicago, asking a judge to stop the marriage. She argued that her daughter, at her tender age, could become the target of schemes, and that Oser was after the money. The judge refused to block it. In April 1923, Mathilde married Max Oser anyway.

Remember that court case. A mother warning a judge that the people around a young heiress might only want her money. Hold on to it, because in just a few years, the same question will be asked about Edith.

Fowler, the eldest surviving son, would cause his own scandal a few years later, when he married Anne Stillman, the divorced mother of one of his own college friends. The headlines followed the family everywhere.

So here was Edith in 1922. Forty-nine years old. Divorced. Her children grown and scattered. Her mother dead. Her father in his eighties and disappointed. The marriage that had once joined two great fortunes now just two names in a newspaper column.

She could have retreated. Lived quietly on what was left. It was still a fortune most people couldn't imagine.

Instead, Edith made the decision that would destroy what remained.

[CHAPTER 7: THE CITY CALLED EDITHTON]

Edith had come back from Zurich with company.

Among the people in her new circle was a young Swiss man named Edwin Krenn, many years her junior, who described himself as an architect. One of her biographers calls that claim dubious at best. He had become her closest companion. With him came his old schoolmate from Zurich, Edward Dato, a Russian Jewish immigrant who had worked as an engineer at International Harvester, the McCormick family company. Together, the three of them launched a real estate venture in Chicago. Edith put up five million dollars. Krenn and Dato, according to Time magazine, put in fifteen hundred dollars each.

Picture America in the middle of the 1920s. The war was over. Jazz poured out of the radio. Cars filled streets that had held horses only a few years earlier. Ordinary people who had never owned a share of stock were buying them on borrowed money, convinced that prices could only go up. In Florida, swamp land was being sold and resold at higher and higher prices to people who had never seen it. It was the age of easy money, and almost no one wanted to hear that it might end.

At first, it looked like the boom of the 1920s would make them all richer. Land prices were rising. Everyone in America seemed to be getting rich on paper. And Edith had a vision far bigger than ordinary property deals.

She wanted to build a city.

On the shore of Lake Michigan, near Racine, Wisconsin, they bought around 1,800 acres and planned an ideal lakefront community. A town with her own name on it. They called it Edithton Beach.

Now think back to that little girl with her account book, earning two cents for every fly. Her father had built his empire by watching every penny and never betting what he couldn't afford to lose.

His daughter was now betting the fortune on a city that existed only on paper.

And at first, the gamble seemed to be working. Land was bought. Plans were drawn. Edith put in more, and then more again. Money flowed out to partners, to schemes, to the people around her. If anyone close to her was questioning the numbers, history doesn't record it.

Here's the uncomfortable question that hangs over these years. Were the people around Edith her partners? Or were they spending her money? The truth is, we don't fully know. Some biographers see Krenn and Dato as genuine believers in a shared dream. Others see two men who found themselves with access to a Rockefeller fortune and very few people willing to say no. This story won't pass judgment where the records are silent. But one thing is clear. By the late 1920s, very little of Edith's wealth was protected from the venture.

And then came October 1929.

On Thursday, October 24th, panic swept the New York Stock Exchange. Five days later, on Tuesday, October 29th, the floor collapsed completely. Black Tuesday. Fortunes that had taken a decade to build on paper were wiped out in an afternoon.

The stock market crashed. The real estate boom collapsed. Land that had been worth a fortune was suddenly worth a fraction. Buyers vanished. Debts did not.

Edithton Beach, the town that would carry her name, was never built.

The woman whose father had never made a careless bet in his life had placed nearly everything on a single dream, at the worst possible moment in American history.

And where was the Rockefeller family? According to accounts of these years, her father, by then deeply cautious about his daughter's ventures, did not rush to rescue them. The man who had paid for Zurich, for the opera, for the villa, would not pay for Edithton Beach.

Maybe he believed she had to learn. Maybe he believed the money would simply disappear like everything else. Or maybe, at ninety years old, he was simply too tired to fight for a daughter who had stopped listening decades ago.

[CHAPTER 8: THE LAST SUITE]

By the early 1930s, Edith could no longer afford to run her own mansion.

The woman who had once lived at 1000 Lake Shore Drive, with footmen behind every chair, moved into a suite at the Drake Hotel in Chicago. Rented rooms. A hotel staff instead of her own. For a Rockefeller, it was a fall almost no one could have imagined.

Try to see it through her eyes. A woman who had grown up with servants, who had married into one great fortune and been born into another, now signing for room service. Walking past strangers in a hotel lobby. Hearing, perhaps, the whispers. That's her. That's the Rockefeller daughter.

Outside the hotel windows, Chicago was sinking into the Great Depression. Banks were failing across the city. Families stood in bread lines a few blocks from Michigan Avenue. Edith had lost most of a fortune, but she still had far more than the people on the street below. And yet, in her own world, she had fallen further than almost anyone.

And there was something else she was carrying. In 1930, she had been diagnosed with breast cancer.

Through 1931 and into 1932, Edith grew weaker. The great heiress spent her final months in hotel rooms looking out over the lake she had once owned a piece of. Villa Turicum, the palace named after Zurich, sat on its bluff to the north, too expensive to live in and too grand to sell.

On August 25, 1932, Edith Rockefeller McCormick died at the Drake Hotel. She was 59 years old. Six days later, she would have turned sixty.

Her father outlived her. John D. Rockefeller was 93 when his daughter died, and he would live almost five more years. The man who recorded every penny he ever spent had to watch his own child spend almost everything and then die before him.

Within a year, the pieces of her world were sold off. Her furnishings, her art, her jewelry, all of it disposed of. In 1953, the mansion at 1000 Lake Shore Drive was sold to a developer and torn down for apartment towers. And Villa Turicum, the palace she barely lived in, sat empty for decades, vandalized and decaying, until it was razed in 1956. Today, only fragments of its gardens remain on the bluff above Lake Michigan.

And her children?

Of Edith's five children, two had died before the age of five. Mathilde died in 1947, in her early forties. Muriel died in 1959, in her fifties. Only one of the five, Fowler, lived past sixty. He would go on to become the head of International Harvester, the company his grandfather had built, and he died in 1973.

Five children. One long life.

But remember the institute in Chicago? The one Edith and Harold founded in 1902, in memory of their little boy who died of scarlet fever?

In 1923, two researchers working at that very institute, George and Gladys Dick, identified the bacterium behind scarlet fever, the illness that had taken her son. A year later, they developed a skin test to show who was at risk, and their work led to early efforts to prevent the disease.

The disease that killed John Rockefeller McCormick was unmasked in a building that carried his name.

Edith rarely spoke about that. But it may be the one investment she made that paid back everything.

[CHAPTER 9: THE ACCOUNT THAT NEVER BALANCED]

Let's go back to the beginning. To a little girl in Cleveland, bent over a tiny account book, writing down two cents for a fly.

Her father taught her that every dollar must be counted. That money was a duty. That a fortune could be built one careful penny at a time.

He was right about the building. But no one ever taught Edith how to protect it. Not from grief. Not from loneliness. Not from the people who came close because of what she had.

Edith Rockefeller McCormick was not simply a spoiled heiress who wasted her money, though that's how the newspapers liked to tell it. She was a mother who buried two children and never truly recovered. A woman who found meaning in Zurich when she couldn't find it at home. A brilliant mind trapped inside expectations she was never allowed to escape. And a daughter who spent her whole life trying to be someone other than the girl with the account book.

She had everything. And in the end, the thing she needed most was the one thing money couldn't buy: someone who could tell her no, and someone she would have listened to.

[FILE LESSON]

Before we close this file, there's one thing in Edith's story worth taking away for our own families.

Her fortune didn't disappear in a single day. It leaked away over years. Through grief, through illness, through people who stood close to the money, and through one enormous bet that left almost nothing protected.

Most of us will never have a Rockefeller fortune. But every family has something worth protecting. A home. Savings. The things we hope to pass on.

So here are the questions Edith's story leaves behind. Is there a clear, up-to-date will? If you became ill, who would be legally trusted to make decisions for you, and would you trust them? Is everything riding on one investment, one person, or one plan? And has anyone in your family actually talked about it out loud?
