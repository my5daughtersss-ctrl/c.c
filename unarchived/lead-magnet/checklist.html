The families behind the fortunes

The Family Legacy
Checklist

What history's richest families got wrong, and simple steps to protect your own.

Edith Rockefeller McCormick was born into the greatest fortune in America. She did not lose it in a single day. It leaked away over years: through grief, through illness, through people who stood close to the money, and through one enormous bet that left almost nothing protected.

Most of us will never hold a Rockefeller fortune. But every family has something worth protecting: a home, savings, the people who depend on us, and the stories we hope to pass on. This checklist turns the hard lessons of famous families into a few calm, practical steps.

HOW TO USE THIS FILE

  1. Work through one section at a time. There's no need to finish in a day.
  2. Tick each box once it's done, or note what's missing.
  3. Bring the completed list to an estate attorney or financial adviser. It will save you time and fees.
  4. Revisit it once a year, and after any major life change.

DOCUMENT IThe Will
When there is no clear will, the law and sometimes the courts decide who gets what.
  • I have a written, signed will that meets the legal requirements where I live. Witness rules vary by state and country.
  • I've named an executor I trust, plus a backup in case they can't serve.
  • I've named a guardian for any minor children or dependents.
  • My executor knows where the original is kept and how to reach my attorney.
  • I've reviewed it since my last big life event: a marriage, divorce, birth, death, move or major purchase.

DOCUMENT IIBeneficiaries
Many accounts pass outside your will, so the beneficiary form is what counts.
  • I've checked the beneficiaries on retirement accounts, life insurance and annuities.
  • I've named backup (contingent) beneficiaries on each.
  • I've asked my bank about payable-on-death or transfer-on-death options.
  • No ex-spouse or late relative is still listed by mistake.
DOCUMENT IIIIf I Can't Speak
Illness can take away your voice. Decide now who speaks for you.
  • Durable power of attorney for my finances.
  • Healthcare proxy and advance directive (a living will).
  • Medical-records permission (in the US, a HIPAA authorization) so loved ones can get information.
  • I chose people who will protect my interests, not simply who is closest to the money.
DOCUMENT IVTrusts: Ask About Them
The great fortunes used trusts for a reason. Many ordinary families can too.
  • I've asked an attorney whether a revocable living trust makes sense for me, for example to avoid probate, add privacy or plan for illness.
  • Special-needs planning is in place if a loved one relies on benefits.
  • I've considered protections for an heir who may struggle with money.
DOCUMENT VProtect the Money
Edith's fortune didn't vanish. It was spent, lent and bet.
  • No single investment, venture or person controls most of what I have.
  • I get a second opinion before any large financial decision.
  • I check the credentials of anyone advising me on money. In the US, FINRA BrokerCheck is free.
  • I know the warning signs of financial exploitation of older adults: sudden pressure, secrecy, new "friends" and unusual withdrawals.

DOCUMENT VIThe Family File
The most painful estates are the ones nobody can find.
  • One master list of accounts, policies, property and debts.
  • Digital accounts noted, with a secure way for loved ones to access them (such as a password manager's emergency access).
  • Where the originals are: the will, deeds, titles and any safe deposit box.
DOCUMENT VIIThe Conversation
Silence is the thread that runs through almost every file we open.
  • My family knows my plans and who to call.
  • I've written a letter of wishes: values, memories, and who should have the heirlooms.
  • I've recorded the family stories before they're lost.
